# joestanley.org design spec

The rules the site must meet. Each rule has an ID. `tests/check_site.py` checks every rule marked **auto** on every build and names the rule when one fails. Rules marked **review** need a person or a review agent. Change a rule here first, then the code, then the check.

Values such as colors and sizes live in the stylesheets (`assets/css/base.css`, and `pm.css` for the syllabus). This spec states what they must achieve, so the checks read the stylesheets rather than copying numbers into a second place.

The look was locked on 18 November 2026, from round 9: one orange ink on white, Newsreader for reading, Libre Franklin for titles and headings, Public Sans for labels, Shantell Sans for the owner's notes, a blended highlighter and a felt-pen circle (H1). V1 and V2 hold it there.

## Color roles

- **C1 auto.** Orange text appears only in page titles (`h1`) and the name on the home page. No other text is orange, except a link while hovered. Words the stylesheet draws, such as a control's label ("Open", "Show answer"), are text too.
- **C2 auto.** No element has an orange or near-black fill behind text, except in the games (J5). No filled label boxes.
- **C3 auto.** Yellow fills appear only on the highlighter (`mark`), answers, the takeaway panel, figure marks and the games' boards (J5): the dice game's waiting piles, the waiting game's weeks of waiting and the ranking game's five worst stores. Glossary terms carry no fill: like every link, they are marked by their underline alone.
- **C4 auto.** Every text and background pair meets WCAG 2.2 AA: 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text, in light and dark. Checked from the stylesheet tokens and by an automated scan of the rendered pages.
- **C5 auto.** Links are distinguishable without color: every link in running text is underlined, on screen and in the PDF, where a link is something to tap too. The underline is a quiet cue, not an accent: a thin grey line (`--link-line`) at 3:1 or better against the page, in light and dark (WCAG 1.4.1, 1.4.11). It turns orange and thicker on hover and keyboard focus, easing in over about 180 milliseconds, in CSS alone, with no script; with reduced motion requested the change is instant (D5). Glossary terms carry a dotted underline, the web's sign for a definition. The controls that fold and unfold (Show answer, Why this source, What a good one looks like) use the same grey line, and so does the line beside a margin note folded into the text. The site's menu, the course bar (G1), the glossary's A to Z rail (G6) and the front door's icons are controls, not running text, and carry no underline: where they sit says they're links.
- **C6 auto.** Words on the highlighter sit wholly on its stroke: no part of a letter reaches past the yellow, in light or dark, in each of the highlighter's three strokes (H1), because dark ink past the stroke meets a dark page. Measured from rendered pixels on the module's one thing and a card's question to carry.
- **C7 auto.** A picture may use colors of its own, beyond the page's ink, orange and highlighter, where its subject calls for one: water is blue, and what was used up, went wrong or fell short is a soft clay (26 November 2026, with F1, which gives each color one job across the set). The owner lifted the lock for pictures on 19 November 2026, as long as their colors stay consistent with the family: soft, and no louder than the highlighter. Every picture color is a named value in `pm.css` (`--fig-` and its job), with a second value for dark screens, and no picture file sets a color itself, so the whole family can be read in one place. Whether a new color belongs in the family is the owner's call, as with any change to a picture (V1).

## Order of weight

- **W1 review.** Each page has one focal point: its title. The yellow highlighter is second.
- **W2 auto.** Section headings (`h2`) are near-black, never orange.
- **W3 auto.** Labels, times and notes are smaller than body text.
- **W4 auto.** Headings look their level: on every page, a heading is never smaller than a heading of a lower level, so the page's outline can be seen as well as heard. (WCAG 1.3.1)

## Margin

- **M1 auto.** On wide screens, and in a module's print, the margin holds only the owner's notes, in the owner's hand (Shantell Sans), each beside the part of the page it is about, or level with the paragraph it names (M4): at the paragraph's top, or at its foot when the owner places it lower (the owner, 21 November 2026). Nothing else sits in the margin: glossary definitions open from the term, and a picture's caption sits under the picture at every width. A note beside a source on the full path sits level with its title and holds no room open: a long one runs on down the margin past its entry, as a note does on a printed page (the owner, 21 November 2026). In print a module's text takes about two thirds of the page's width and the margin column the rest (the owner, 21 November 2026); a paragraph with a note never breaks across pages, and every note prints whole, on one page, beside its part. Every note in the margin starts at the same left edge, so the notes read as one column. On phones a note folds into the text under the heading of its part, or under its paragraph, in the typeset face, marked by a thin line at its left. In print on Start, The Compass, a card or the glossary, which keep the page's full width, a note folds in the same way, in the owner's hand, so his notes look alike everywhere in the PDF (the owner, 21 November 2026). Wherever a note folds into the text, a label, "Sidenote:", sits on its own line above it in the label type, so a reader who can't see a margin still knows the note is an aside in his voice; in the margin, where its place says so, the label is hidden from the eye (the owner chose the word on 21 November 2026).
- **M2 auto.** Margin notes never overlap each other or the reading column, with each module's full path open, where every note shows. In print, two notes one above the other keep a gap (`--sp-3` at least) wider than a break inside a note, so they read as two notes (the owner, 21 November 2026).
- **M3 auto.** Screen readers meet each margin note once, introduced by the same label, "Sidenote:", wherever it sits (M1): right after the heading of the part it is about, or, for a step or a source, after its description, so a title stays with its time, or, for a paragraph, right after that paragraph.
- **M4 auto.** Every part of every unit has a preserved slot for a margin note: each section, each step and source, each game, and each glossary term. A unit's slots are listed in reading order in `data/pm/margin/<unit>.yaml`, empty until the owner writes in one; an empty slot prints nothing. A slot holds one note, beside its part's heading, or a list of notes, each of which may name one paragraph of its part by a few of its words (`beside`) and sit level with it (`at: foot` puts it at the paragraph's foot). The file lists every part of its unit and nothing else, and a note that names words no paragraph of its part holds fails the check, so a note can never be lost to a renamed part or a reworded paragraph. `tools-pm/margin-slots.py` adds the slots for a new or changed unit and keeps the notes already written.

## Type

- **T1 auto.** Every font the stylesheets reference exists in `static/fonts/` with its license file, and every font loads on the pages that use it.
- **T2 auto.** Reading lines stay at or under 80 characters on every screen width.
- **T3 auto.** No em dashes in content, drafts, templates or styles, or in the voice guide, `VOICE.md`. The voice guide is kept in the private JoeStanley/pm-sources with the rest of the working record (the owner, 22 November 2026), so the check reads it where pm-sources sits beside this repository, as it does in every working session, and notes that it couldn't where it doesn't, as on GitHub.
- **T9 auto.** American punctuation, as the owner chose on 22 November 2026. A comma or a period that ends a quoted phrase or a quoted title goes inside the closing quotation mark, single or double: chapter 1, "The Underpinning," and chapter 7, "Making it Happen." A quoted question or exclamation takes no comma or period after it. Colons and semicolons stay outside. It holds in every unit, built or draft, as its tab shows it (X2), and in the owner's margin notes, where a session makes it as a house-rule fix and tells him. The check reads double quotation marks, since a closing single one looks the same as an apostrophe; a single one inside a double one is caught by the double.
- **T4 auto.** Text comes in five sizes on screen and four in print, each named for its job in `base.css`: small (labels, times, notes, controls), body (reading text, and headings below section level), heading (section headings, and the question under a title), title (page titles) and, on screen only, the name on the front door. Every piece of text a reader meets is set at one of them, on every page, at phone and desktop widths and in print. Next sizes differ by at least 1.2 times, so each reads as a different size. Stylesheets name the sizes rather than writing numbers. Text inside pictures belongs to the picture and is exempt.
- **T5 auto.** Each face has a fixed set of weights, each with a job: Libre Franklin 800 (titles and the site name) and 700 (headings); Newsreader 400, roman and italic, for reading, and 600, roman and italic, for bold words and citations; Public Sans 400 and 600 for labels and controls, and 400 italic for the title of a work cited in small type; Shantell Sans 400 and 600 for margin notes, and 400 for a line edit in the owner's hand (H2). Four weights in all, each doing the same job in every face: 800 titles, 700 headings, 600 bold, 400 text. No other pairing of face and weight appears.
- **T6 auto.** Line spacing comes from three named values: tight (titles), heading (headings and the question under a title) and text (everything else). Text spacing is at least 1.5, so running text of any size, notes included, keeps the line spacing WCAG recommends (1.4.8).
- **T7 auto.** Sizes follow the reader's text-size setting: on screen, the type and spacing scales are set in rem, so a reader who sets a larger default text size in their browser gets larger text and proportional space, not only those who zoom. (WCAG 1.4.4)

## Spacing

- **T8 auto.** Vertical space between and inside blocks (margins, padding and gaps) comes from one spacing scale of eight steps, on screen and in print. Print uses the same steps on a smaller unit, so the printed page keeps the screen's proportions. Exempt: the highlighter, a mark inside a line of text, and the step circles, whose place is computed from their title (R6).

## Hand-drawn marks

- **H1 auto.** The marks vary as a hand does. The highlighter is a felt pen's stroke with slanted ends over a slightly wobbly middle; a step circle is a felt-pen loop that overshoots its start. Each comes in three variants, picked by where the mark sits rather than at random, so the page is the same on every visit and in print. Neighbouring steps never share a circle, and the highlighted lines of a module and its cards do not all share one stroke. Every variant meets the same rules as the others: C6 for each highlighter variant, R6 for each circle.
- **H2 auto.** A line edit in the owner's hand. Where the owner corrects a word in the text, the old word stays in its line, scratched out with a pen stroke that leaves it readable, and the new words sit just above it in his hand (Shantell Sans), the way a correction is written on a printed page. They are written as a right-handed writer's quick line edit is: they start just before the old word and run to the right, rising between 3 and 6 degrees. The old word keeps no more space beside it than any other word in the line: where the new words are longer, they reach out over the next word instead of pushing the words apart. Where the old word ends a line, the new words shift left to end with the line (the owner, 20 November 2026). The scratch comes in three strokes, picked by where the edit sits, and takes the page's ink in light and dark. The new words never touch the line above or run past the screen, at phone and desktop widths. Read aloud, the new words come first and the old word follows, announced as crossed out (A10), which is also the order the eye meets them in, top to bottom (A6). Where the drawn stroke cannot show (D4), the old word carries a plain line through it. It prints as it shows on screen. It is for the owner's own corrections, used sparingly. The owner asked for it on 20 November 2026, for the quote that opens the Start page.

## Pictures

A concept card's picture is a small diagram, and the cards' pictures are one set. Two reviewers who drew none of them, an information designer and an illustrator, read all of them on 26 November 2026, and found them close in ink and type and apart in everything else: what the yellow meant, what a dotted line meant, arrowheads, dots, corners, and how two cases were compared. The owner asked that the pictures "adopt a consistent behavior of information display", feel part of the site's family, and may use more colors on the way (26 November 2026). F1 is that behavior, and F2 holds the pictures readable on a phone. Published practice says the same: hold the encoding constant and let only the content change, give each mark one meaning, and highlight one thing.

- **F1 auto.** One grammar for every picture. A picture file (`assets/figures/*.svg`) is drawn 360 units wide and uses only the marks `pm.css` names for pictures, each with one job, and sets no color, size or weight itself (C7).
  - **Yellow is the key moment:** the one place the card says to look or act. Each picture has one, and only one: a single mark, or up to three like marks that make one moment (the three facts of one answer). It is always a rectangle behind or between things (a highlighter behind words, a band behind line work, a block behind one point, one part of a bar), never a shape with a meaning of its own, and something inked sits on it or bounds it. It covers no more than 8% of the picture. Its label is bold, sits on it or beside it, and names the yellow part, not the whole it belongs to, or says the one thing to do there. A highlighter behind words is 18 units high and reaches 4 past the words at each end; a block behind one point is 28 units square, centred on the point. Words on the yellow take the highlighter's own ink in light and dark, as highlighted words do in the text.
  - **Lines.** A thin ink line is something real that moves or holds, or an axis. A heavy ink line is the line the picture is about: a series, a path of work or change, a line you draw or one that divides (the Trust Equation). Several like paths are thin (Agency and Coherence), and a drawn object keeps its own weight (the tap). A dotted grey line is what isn't really there: with an arrowhead it is information going back, and without one it is not promised or not certain, and a dotted outline is a thing that may never exist. A thin grey line is a guide, scaffolding only: a divider, a reference, a leader or a bracket that ties a label to its mark. Line weight carries data only as a declared ramp (Access Patterns). Where a second series runs beside the heavy one, it is thin ink: it is as real as the first (Goodhart's Law). Two paths from one start are one chart, not a pair: heavy for the path the picture is about, thin for the other, each named beside its own line (the Concept Champion). A series runs along a drawn axis; where a picture claims no values it may leave the axes out, a named dot marking the start, and series that end at the same time end at the same place across.
  - **One arrowhead:** a triangle 10 units across and 9 long, in its shaft's color, ink on a solid line and grey on a dotted one. An arrow reads "leads to": a flow, a next step, a cause, or what comes back. A line without one is a link, not a direction; a series along an axis, and lines that meet at one point, need none.
  - **Dots and boxes.** A dot is one thing: 4 units in radius where things are counted, 5.5 where it has a name, 14 where it carries a numeral (R6), with a ring. A named dot in a column is a list. On two axes it is plotted: where it sits is data, its label moves and not the dot, and two dots share a place on an axis only when their values do. On one axis, dots whose values fall in the same narrow range pile up: the range is the same along a row, and the caption says "about". A box with paper inside is a thing that holds, a system, a team, a container or an answer, with corners rounded by 6 units, and it may take its subject's outline (a tub, a pipe, an arrow). A quantity, a bar or a counted square, has square corners, and a counted square where nothing went wrong is empty, with a quiet edge.
  - **Words** come in three ranks: bold for a named thing, and for a number beside its bar; regular ink for an item in a list or a series; regular grey for a gloss, an axis or a note. A gloss sits under its label and starts in lower case. Where the label rests on a line, the gloss may sit on the label's far side from the line (the Trust Equation's top row) or across the line from it, at its other end or under its axis (the Mission Excuse, the Law of Small Numbers), with no other words between. A label sits nearer its own mark than any other. Words one person says to another are in quotation marks, and a bold label starts with a capital unless it is a value, quoted or after its field's name (the Last Time); a note that stands alone, or an axis end, starts with a capital. A named dot's name is bold or regular ink, never grey. A picture carries at most one footnote, grey, at its lower left, for what the drawing can't show: a key, a caveat or the consequence. Where the foot holds an axis or the drawing itself, a key or a caveat may head the picture as a label with its gloss (the Law of Small Numbers, Read the Errors).
  - **Two cases are drawn as one drawing twice,** and only what differs changes. A cause and its mirror are such a pair too: side by side, the cause on the left, one arrow between. Things that run left to right stack, the plain case on top; things that run top to bottom sit side by side, the weaker case on the left. Each case is headed or footed by a bold label with its gloss, in the same place in each.
  - **Color beyond ink and yellow** has one job each, the same in every picture (C7): blue is water, and clay is what was used up, went wrong or fell short, as a quantity (a bar or counted squares), and everything a picture counts as such is clay. A colored fill carries an ink edge or a label, so color is never the only cue, and yellow never stands in clay's place.
  - **The frame.** Nothing comes nearer than 12 units to a side, and a drawing narrower than the frame is centred in it; the first line of words sits near 18 from the top, and the last about 8 from the foot, so every picture sits in its panel the same way.
- **F2 auto.** A picture reads on a phone. At 390 pixels a picture runs the full width of its panel, through the panel's padding (the owner, 27 November 2026: the pictures never carry margin notes), and no word in it is set under 12 pixels there. No two pieces of text in a picture overlap, none runs out of the picture, and none sits on the yellow without the highlighter's ink. Measured in a browser, on every card.

## Layout and responsiveness

- **R1 auto.** No sideways scrolling at 320, 390, 768, 1024, 1280 and 1920 pixels wide, or on a landscape phone (844 by 390).
- **R2 auto.** At 200% zoom (a 640-pixel-wide viewport standing in for 1280), no sideways scrolling and no text cut off.
- **R3 auto.** Below 1024 pixels the margin folds into the text; from 1024 pixels up it sits beside the reading column. Checked on a build with a sample note in every slot.
- **R4 auto.** On the home page the photo sits beside the name on wide screens and above it on narrow ones.
- **R5 auto.** Interactive targets are at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels; the home page's icon links are at least 44 by 44.
- **R6 auto.** A number set in a circle reads clearly and sits in the middle. The numeral is at least 40% of the circle's height, and its centre is within 3% of the circle's size from the circle's centre, on screen and in the PDF itself. This covers the short-path step circles, the dice game's stations and the numbered steps in concept-card pictures. A step circle also lines up with its task: its centre is within 5% of its size from the middle of the capitals on the first line of the step's title. Measured from rendered pixels, on screen and in the PDF rendered at 600 dpi, because the drawn ring, the shapes of the digits and the way each renderer rounds positions decide what the eye sees. A circle never prints in pieces: when a step moves to a new page, no part of its ring is left behind on the page before. A dice-game station's number is text set in a drawn disc, and a browser puts text on whole pixels, so how it sits depends on where the page above happens to end: it is measured with the disc on the pixel grid, and on a page where the disc lands between pixels it may sit up to a pixel off.
- **R7 auto.** With the browser's default text size doubled (32 pixels instead of 16), no page scrolls sideways at 390 or 1280 pixels, no text is cut off, and margin notes do not overlap. (WCAG 1.4.4, 1.4.10)
- **R9 auto.** The site's menu. Its sections are ReadMe, On Judgment and Games: the syllabus's label in the menu is On Judgment, everywhere, while the course bar keeps the syllabus's full title, On Product Judgment (G1; the owner, 25 November 2026). From 46rem (736 pixels) up, on every page, the name and every section share one line, the current section in bold over the orange line, with room on that line for a fourth section, Writing, once it has a post. Below 46rem the line holds the name, the current section, in bold over its orange line, so the mark that says where you are shows on a phone too, and a Menu button; the front door, which is no section, shows the name and the button. The button opens the other sections in a list that hangs from the header's right edge, over the page and the course bar, and reaches left only a step past where the menu starts, so its sections line up under the current one and a reader's finger stays where the menu is; the current section is beside the button already, so the list leaves it out (the owner, 25 November 2026, on seeing the first build). On the front door the list holds every section and is as wide as its words need. A thin rule edges it on its left and at its foot. Each of its rows is its link, the list's full width, so a tap beside a section's name still lands on it (the owner, 25 November 2026). The button is a real button with `aria-expanded`. Once opened, the list stays open until the reader presses the button again, presses Escape (which puts focus back on the button), taps or clicks outside the menu, the course bar included, moves focus out of it, or scrolls the page. A scroll doesn't close it while focus is inside it, or while the list runs past the window's foot, so a reader tabbing through it or reading at a large text size never loses it. At 390 pixels the current section and the button are each 44 pixels tall, and so is each section in the list. Without scripts every section shows, and the line wraps where it must, rather than scroll. The owner chose the button on 25 November 2026 (card menu-row), with Writing in mind, over a shortened label on one line; on 20 November he had kept every section in view, and he holds no strong view on it. Writing stays out of the menu, and isn't built, until it has a post (the owner, 22 November 2026).
- **R8 auto.** The site works the same in Safari's engine (WebKit) and in Firefox, not only in Chrome. In both, at 390 pixels (in WebKit, as an iPhone) and 1280: no page scrolls sideways or runs text off the screen (R1), every size, weight, line spacing and gap is on the scales (T4 to T6, T8), and the enlarged-text and text-spacing checks hold (R7, A13). A failure there is reported under its own rule and names the engine; R8 itself fails only if an engine cannot run. The step circles and the PDF are checked in Chrome, which makes the PDF.
- **R10 auto.** A module's full path spans its reading column, like the rest of the module (the owner, 21 November 2026). Its Open and Close label belongs to the full path's own heading and draws nothing outside it, so it takes no width from the sources below, sits on no rule and pushes no source down, and a note beside a source lines up with every other note (M1). A note beside the full path's heading pushes no source down either, however long it runs: the first source's note waits below it, as each source's note waits below the one above (the owner, 21 November 2026). The check builds the site with the longest sample note beside every full path's heading. No control inside the full path takes the label: each "Why this source" keeps its own mark. The label sits at the left, under the heading, where every other control's label sits, not across the column at the right, and looks like "Show answer": ink, with the grey underline that turns orange on hover (C1, C5; the owner, 25 November 2026).

## Graceful degradation

- **D1 auto.** Without JavaScript every page reads fully: each game (J5) shows its rules, one recorded run and the takeaway; answers and source reasons stay folded but open by tapping; glossary terms link to the glossary.
- **D2 auto.** Without the dialog feature (`showModal`), a glossary term still takes the reader to its definition in the glossary. With it, tapping a term opens the definition in place.
- **D3 auto.** Without web fonts, nothing scrolls sideways and nothing overlaps.
- **D4 auto.** Features newer browsers add (drawn masks, `:has()`) are guarded, so an older browser shows a plain version rather than a broken one.
- **D5 auto.** With reduced motion requested, nothing animates on its own, a game finishes at once, and a link's change on hover or focus is instant.

## Interaction and accessibility

- **A1 auto.** The automated accessibility scan (axe-core, WCAG 2.2 A and AA rules) finds no violations on any page, including the single document `/pm/all/`, in light or dark, at 390 or 1280 pixels.
- **A2 auto.** Every element that takes keyboard focus shows a visible focus ring.
- **A3 auto.** Icon-only links have accessible names.
- **A4 review.** A screen reader pass reads each page in a sensible order with sensible names.
- **A5 auto.** A glossary term opens a modal dialog named by the term, with the definition as its description. Opening it moves focus inside. While it is open, Tab and Shift+Tab never reach the page behind it; the browser may offer its own address bar, as it does for every modal dialog. Escape and Close return focus to the term. (WCAG 2.4.3, 4.1.2)
- **A6 auto.** A screen reader meets things in the order the eye does: in the main content, nothing sits above an element that comes before it in the source. Figure captions are the one exception, because a caption names its figure. (WCAG 1.3.2)
- **A7 auto.** Names make sense read aloud. No name starts with punctuation. A disclosure's name carries no state word the stylesheet adds ("Open", "Close"). A tick box says what ticking does ("Mark done: ..."). A label that runs straight into what it introduces, a game's counters and each picture's title end with punctuation, so the voice pauses. (WCAG 4.1.2, 1.3.1)
- **A8 auto.** What a game shows on its board is also given in words. After a run, the result says what the board shows, matching it: in the dice game, how many items wait at each station; in the waiting game, the week each job went live, what the waiting cost and what each estimate turned out to be; in the ranking game, how many of the five worst stores are small; once the funnel is drawn, which stores fall outside it; and in the trial, how many of the five worst have the fix. A change of setup is announced. The key to the board is hidden from screen readers along with the board. (WCAG 1.1.1, 4.1.3)
- **A9 auto.** Focus is never dropped. Play keeps focus while the game runs, and choosing a unit in the single document moves focus to that unit's title. (WCAG 2.4.3)
- **A10 auto.** Struck-out text is announced as crossed out, because its meaning can depend on it. (WCAG 1.3.1)
- **A11 auto.** Headings never skip a level on the way down. (WCAG 1.3.1)
- **A12 auto.** Text that screen readers read never points to a color as the way to find something. Checked by looking for color names in what screen readers read. A capitalized color word right after another capitalized word is part of a name, such as Charles Green, one of *The Trusted Advisor*'s authors, and isn't counted (22 November 2026). (WCAG 1.3.3)
- **A13 auto.** Readers can override text spacing (line height 1.5, paragraph spacing 2 times the text size, letter spacing 0.12 and word spacing 0.16 times the text size) without losing anything: at 320 and 1280 pixels no page scrolls sideways, no text is cut off by its box, and margin notes do not overlap. (WCAG 1.4.12)

## Print

- **P1 auto.** Each unit prints on its own Letter pages and keeps to its share: Start on no more than two pages, now that it lists thirteen modules in their four parts (S20), a module on no more than three, a concept card on one, The Compass on three, its call walked twice (S21), or on four until a PDF is published, noted and not failed, now that it names what the course leaves out (the owner, 26 November 2026, over trimming its walks or leaving the list out of print), and the glossary on three, now that Modules 6 and 7 bring nine more terms (31 in all). Until a PDF is published, the glossary may run past its three pages, listed in the check's notes, so every term a module brings goes in (the owner, 22 November 2026, when Modules 8 and 9 brought ten more, 41 in all; Modules 10 and 11 bring eight more, 49 in all, and Know Who It's For two, 51 in all; 56 in all by 26 November 2026, the last two stop rule and last responsible moment, each explained where it's named and now in the glossary too). A module's full path prints outside its three pages (the owner, 20 November 2026): the three pages hold the module, its full path starts within them, and the full path, with the answers and the sources' reasons that print after it, may run onto two more pages, five at most, now that a module's text takes two thirds of the page beside its margin (the owner, 21 November 2026). Until a PDF is published, the website comes first and the print second (the owner, 22 November 2026, who moved both allowances from the site's launch to the first published PDF the same day, since fitting the print waits until he wants one): a module whose own text runs onto a fourth page, or that prints on six pages in all, is listed in the check's notes and does not fail, so the print never cuts what the site says.
