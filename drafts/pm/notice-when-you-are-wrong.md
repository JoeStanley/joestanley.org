## Why this matters

Every plan rests on assumptions, and some of yours are probably wrong today. You don't know which yet. This module is about finding them early, in your thinking and your team's, and saying what you found so the people who decide can act.

## Terms you will meet

- **Cognitive bias:** a predictable error in thinking that everyone makes, not a sign of carelessness.
- **Hindsight bias:** after something goes wrong, the feeling that you knew it all along.
- **Outcome bias:** judging a decision by how bad the result was, not by what was known when it was made.
- **Ladder of inference:** Chris Argyris's model of how we climb from what we observe, through the data we pick out, the meaning we add and the beliefs we form, to action, usually without noticing the steps.
- **Left-hand column:** an exercise from Argyris and Donald Schön. Beside what was said in a conversation, you write what you thought and felt but didn't say.
- **Espoused theory and theory-in-use:** what we say guides our behavior, and what actually does, as others can see from our actions.
- **Single-loop learning:** changing your actions to reach the same goal, like a thermostat switching the heat on and off.
- **Double-loop learning:** questioning the assumptions and goals behind the actions, not just the actions.
- **Psychological safety:** Amy Edmondson's term for a shared belief that it's safe to take interpersonal risks at work, such as raising a concern or admitting a mistake.
- **Risk storming:** Simon Brown's workshop technique. People mark risks on a diagram of the system alone and in silence, then compare and prioritize them together.
- **Premortem:** Gary Klein's technique of assuming a project has already failed and asking the team why.
- **Design crit:** short for design critique, a review that raises questions and concerns about work in progress without attacking the people doing it.
- **Architecture decision record (ADR):** a short note recording one decision and why you made it; see the decision record in Module 8, Decide and Set Direction.
- **Request for comment (RFC):** a document that describes a problem and a few possible solutions, shared for comment before anyone decides.
- **Topic position and stress position:** Gopen and Swan's names for the start of a sentence, where readers expect familiar information and learn who or what the sentence is about, and the end, where they expect what is new and important.
- **SCR and bottom line up front:** two ways of putting the point first. SCR (situation, complication, resolution) is covered in the SCR card, and bottom line up front in the Fractal Storytelling card.

## What to look for

- **Your judgments feel like facts.** On p. 395, notice Argyris's point: we reach them so fast we treat them as concrete. Batista's post gives you a question for each rung, so you can climb back down to the data.
- **Bad outcomes rewrite history.** In Allspaw's list on p. 389, hindsight bias and outcome bias are the two to watch in any review. Listen for "they should have", and for blame that grows with the size of the damage.
- **Fixing the action is not fixing the goal.** Pages 401 and 402 contrast tuning the method with questioning the assumptions behind the goal. Retuning a streaming service's recommendations to lift watch time is the first; asking whether watch time keeps subscribers is the second.
- **Silence can become an organization's habit.** Argyris's 1977 example on p. 400 is a norm against challenging policies top management likes, and a second norm that nobody mentions the first. Edmondson's definition on p. 403 describes the opposite: people say what they see, and the bar stays high. For when to voice or hold back your own view, see the Curious and Opinionated card in Module 0, Own Your Role.
- **Assume it has already failed.** Klein's premortem on p. 456 asks what did go wrong, not what might, and that makes it safer for doubters to speak. Risk storming on p. 455 adds a second protection: everyone writes risks alone, in silence, before anyone discusses them.
- **Consequences before verdicts.** Nygard (p. 491 and his post) asks you to list how the future will differ before calling anything a pro or a con. Then ask what follows from each consequence.

## Apply it to your own work

1. Write a left-hand column, in about 15 minutes. Pick a recent meeting where you held back a concern, or where a decision went a way you doubted. Everyone has one. Follow the steps on deck p. 396: what was said goes in the right column, what you thought in the left. Then take one left-column thought down the ladder of inference: what did you actually see or hear, and what did you add? Rewrite it as one sentence you could have said, starting from the data.
2. Run a premortem on your own work, in about 15 minutes. Imagine it's a year from now and your main initiative has failed. Alone and in silence, as risk storming asks, write at least five plausible reasons why. Rate each one low, medium or high for how likely it is and how much damage it would do (p. 455). For the top one, name an early signal you could watch for in the next month. A first pass from what you already know is fine. To test it, ask a colleague who knows the work, or whoever would see it break first, such as an on-call engineer: "If this failed, what would you bet the reason was?"
3. Tell one story at three sizes, in about 30 minutes. Take the top risk from exercise 2 and turn it into a clear ask of your manager, such as a decision, a person or some time. Write it as one sentence (5 minutes). Next, write an elevator pitch as one SCR paragraph of under 100 words, where the resolution is your ask (8 minutes). Then write a one-page memo with the parts on p. 493: the problem, two or three approaches, your recommendation, and open questions (12 minutes). In it, list consequences before you call them pros or cons. Last, revise the memo's opening paragraph with Gopen and Swan in mind: does each sentence start with something the reader knows and end on what's new? (5 minutes). Send the page to your manager and ask what's missing.

## Check your own thinking

The shift this module is after is from "how do I show I am right?" to "how would I find out I am wrong, and what does the person deciding need to read first?" Test yourself against it: of your next three updates or decision requests, count how many state the ask in the first two sentences and name one way you could be wrong. You're aiming for three out of three. If you'd like a sounding board, take your premortem list and your one-page memo to your manager or a mentor.

# SCR (Situation, Complication, Resolution)

## The idea

SCR opens a message so the reader knows right away why it matters and what you want. The situation is what the reader already knows and accepts; the complication is what changed or went wrong; the resolution is what you recommend or ask for. The pattern is Barbara Minto's, developed in The Pyramid Principle while she edited consultants' reports at McKinsey.

Example, for a team that owns a school gradebook:

- **S:** Our gradebook syncs class rosters from each district's records system every night.
- **C:** Our largest district moves to a new records system in August, and our integration vendor can't ship a connector before school starts.
- **R:** Approve a weekly manual roster import for one term, so teachers have their classes on day one while the vendor builds the connector.

## Why a PM should care

- **Decision makers read the top.** Minto's rule is to give the summarizing idea before the ideas it summarizes. A reader who agrees can stop there, and one who doubts knows what to test in the rest.
- **The situation buys agreement.** Start from something the reader already accepts and you're on the same side before you ask for anything. Keep it short and uncontroversial.
- **The complication is the case for change.** Without one, the reader has no reason to act. Tell it as a short story: what changed, and why the current course won't work anymore.
- **It shows you what you do not know.** If you can't write the complication in one sentence, you may not understand the problem yet, and it's better to find that out at your desk than in the meeting. If the resolution doesn't answer the complication, the argument doesn't hold.
- **It works at every size.** The same three moves open an email, a status update, a note raising a risk or the first slide of a deck.

## How to spot it at work

- Updates open with background and get to the ask in the last paragraph, or never.
- A decision meeting spends its first 20 minutes agreeing on what the problem is.
- Slides are titled with topics, such as "Q3 metrics", instead of what the reader should conclude.
- Replies ask "so what do you need from me?"

## What it does not say

SCR shapes the opening, not the whole document. In Minto's pyramid the supporting points still sit beneath the main point, each group summed up by the point above it. It isn't a way to soften bad news either, because the honest complication often is the bad news. When you don't have a recommendation yet, the resolution can be the question you need help with, stated plainly. And it isn't only a consulting habit: writing teachers Joseph Williams and Joseph Bizup open the same way, with shared context, then a problem (a condition and its cost), then the main point.

## One question to carry into your work

For the decision you most need from someone this month, what's the complication, in one sentence they'd agree with?

# Fractal Storytelling

## The idea

Fractal storytelling means telling the same story at several sizes, each one complete at its own size. This syllabus uses five: one sentence, an elevator pitch, a one-page memo, a 20-minute deck and an hour-long deep dive. Each carries the whole arc, from situation to complication to resolution. Only the depth of detail changes, the way a fractal repeats its shape at every zoom level. The name and the sizes are this syllabus's own framing, drawn from practice and not from a published method. A related habit is bottom line up front: the main point goes first, as in the US Army's standard.

Example, for a developer platform team pausing a redesign.

- **One sentence:** "Pause the API usage dashboard redesign for six weeks, because the pricing team hasn't yet settled what the new plans will charge for."
- **Elevator version:** "The dashboard redesign is on track for May. The pricing team may change what we charge for, and building before they decide risks rework. I recommend pausing six weeks for their answer, and I need your help getting it on their calendar."

## Why a PM should care

- **Each audience has a different attention budget.** An executive may give you one sentence in a corridor, and an engineer may give you an hour. Each should get the whole story, not the opening of the long one.
- **Each size tests the others.** If you can't say it in one sentence, your deck may be a tour of facts. If the sentence can't grow into an hour, it may be a slogan.
- **Retelling shows you what you missed.** The deck notes that unfolding a design's story for one audience, then another, pulls you toward their point of view, and their questions bring hidden assumptions into view (p. 398).
- **Consistent sizes build trust.** When the corridor version and the memo agree, people can act on the short one without waiting for the long one.

## How to spot it at work

- The "executive summary" turns out to be the long document's opening paragraph, so a reader who stops there misses the conclusion.
- Someone asks for the short version, and the answer takes five minutes.
- The memo and the deck for the same decision recommend slightly different things.
- People learn about a decision only from the hour-long review.

## What it does not say

It doesn't mean "cut the long version down". An editor can trim a news story from the bottom because the inverted pyramid has already ordered it by importance. A decision story cut short usually loses its resolution, so write each size fresh around the same arc. It does not say every story needs all five sizes: a small decision may need only the sentence and the page. And shorter isn't easier. The one-sentence version is often the hardest to write, and the long versions are where the evidence and the doubts belong.

## One question to carry into your work

Could a colleague outside your team hear the one-sentence version of your main initiative once and repeat it accurately?

# Double-Loop Learning

## The idea

Chris Argyris called organizational learning "a process of detecting and correcting error." Single-loop learning corrects the error and keeps the goal, like a thermostat switching the heat on when the room gets cold. Double-loop learning questions the goal itself, and the norms and assumptions behind it. A double-loop thermostat would ask, "Why am I set at 68 degrees?"

His 1977 *Harvard Business Review* article tells of Product X, which lost more than $100 million. At least five people knew it was in trouble six years before it was dropped, but their memos were softened and cut on the way up. Argyris called the cause "norms embedded within norms": don't confront policies top management is excited about, and don't openly confront the rule that tells you so.

## Why a PM cares

- **Most of your feedback loops are single loop.** Retros, experiments and incident reviews tune the method and leave the goal alone. A claims team that keeps cutting days off insurance payouts may never ask whether speed is what claimants wanted. You're often the one placed to ask.
- **Bad news waits for a fix.** The people closest to Product X knew "bad news would not be well received at the upper levels if it was not accompanied by suggestions for positive action." If engineers and support tell you first, that's where the softening starts.
- **Successful people learn worst from failure.** Argyris found that when professionals' "single-loop learning strategies go wrong, they become defensive, screen out criticism, and put the 'blame' on anyone and everyone but themselves." Most of us do it. When a launch misses, check whether every reason you list sits outside your team.
- **Use his other tools.** The left-hand column and the ladder of inference, earlier in this module, help you find the assumption under a goal.

## How to spot it

- Every retro ends with a tweak to the process. None has asked "should we be doing this at all?"
- On a telecom network rollout, every slip brings a plan to speed up the crews, and nobody asks whether the coverage target still fits.
- The real reason a project is struggling gets said in the corridor after the meeting.

## What it does not say

It does not say single-loop learning is bad: Argyris argued that the capacity for double loop "does not inhibit single loop learning; indeed, it usually helps it." It doesn't mean reopening the goal every week. And an off-site where everyone levels with each other won't fix it for long. Of such workshops he wrote, "After a month or so back at home, the spirit seems to wane."

**One question to carry:** Which assumption behind your team's goal hasn't been questioned since it was set, and who would tell you if it were wrong?
