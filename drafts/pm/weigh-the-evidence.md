About 92 minutes: 55 reading, 37 doing (a 25-minute exercise and a 12-minute optional one).

**If you only do one thing:** Take one decision in front of you, list the evidence you're leaning on, and write next to each piece who's missing from it.

## Why this matters

You'll meet both mistakes in the same week. On Monday, one vivid story from a customer, or one loud sales call, turns the roadmap. On Thursday, a dashboard settles an argument because it has decimals in it, though nobody can say who's in the data or who isn't. Both feel responsible at the time. The aim is to be data-informed, not data-driven: use the numbers and the stories for what each can tell you, and keep the decision yours.

Numbers tell you how much and how often, across many people, but only about what someone chose to measure. Stories tell you why, and what nobody thought to measure, but only about the people who told them. Fields where the stakes are people's lives have learned the limits of each. Eileen Munro's review of child protection in England found that targets and indicators had pulled attention to what was easy to count, and that a professional's intuition still has to be challenged rather than simply trusted. Psychologists comparing formulas with experts found that on repeated predictions with clear outcomes, a simple formula does as well as an experienced judge or better. Policy researchers, meanwhile, find that what worked in one place often fails in the next.

When the numbers and the stories disagree, look into why before you pick a side. In a 2023 interview, Jeff Bezos said that "when the data and the anecdotes disagree, the anecdotes are usually right", and gave his reason: "it's usually that you're not measuring the right thing." His example was a metric in a weekly business review that showed customer service waits of under a minute, while customers complained of far longer. He called the number from the meeting and, as he recalls it, waited more than ten minutes.

## Do

**Weigh the evidence for one decision (25 minutes).** Pick a decision in front of you, such as whether to retire a feature few people use, or whether a delivery app should change how it batches orders. List every piece of evidence you're leaning on, one line each: the support tickets, the customer calls, the dashboard, the case study from another company. Mark each one a story or a number. Next to each, answer three questions in a few words. Who's missing from it? How was it collected? And what would have to hold here for it to carry over? Then write one sentence on what would change your mind. Outside the 25 minutes, take the page to someone who'd argue the other side, and ask which piece they'd challenge first.

**Optional: find the stories behind one number (12 minutes).** Pick a number on your dashboard that moved last month, such as failed payments or average time to book. Find three individual cases behind the move: three orders, say, or three tickets. Read them. Does what happened to those people match what the number says happened? If it doesn't, write down what the number isn't measuring.

## Check yourself

Answer in your head, then check.

Seven of the ten people in your usability test liked the new flow. Can you say 70 percent of your users like it?

**Answer:** No. Ten people picked for a test don't stand for all your users, and a percentage claims a precision the method can't give. Budiu's point is that in a qualitative test you aim for insights, not numbers. Ask instead why the three who struggled struggled.

After your worst week of complaints, the team changes the checkout, and the next week is better. Did the change work?

**Answer:** You can't tell yet. A worst week is usually followed by a better one anyway: that's regression to the mean, from the Law of Small Numbers card. Compare with customers who didn't get the change (Module 6, Change Safely, covers how), or give it more weeks.

A competitor's case study says a feature lifted retention by 20 percent. What do you ask before you copy it?

**Answer:** Cartwright's question: how would it lead to that result here, step by step, and what would support or derail each step for your customers?

# The McNamara Fallacy

## The idea

In 1971, the market researcher Daniel Yankelovich warned a room of sales executives about what he called "the McNamara fallacy", after Robert McNamara, once the president of Ford. It runs in four steps:

1. "The first step is to measure whatever can be easily measured. This is okay as far as it goes."
2. "The second step is to disregard that which can't be easily measured or give it an arbitrary quantitative value. This is artificial and misleading."
3. "The third step is to presume that what can't be measured easily really isn't very important. This is blindness."
4. "The fourth step is to say that what can't be easily measured really doesn't exist. This is suicide."

His own story was a study his firm did for Ford on small cars. It found nine different things a small car meant to buyers, and only one of them was size: others included cheap running costs, function over styling, and a car that said something about its owner. Ford's management kept the ownership and demographic numbers and discarded the nine meanings, because they couldn't be quantified as rigorously. Later events, he said, showed that those meanings pointed to "an important future direction of the car market".

## Why a PM cares

- **Your dashboard is step one.** It counts what was easy to measure, such as clicks and sessions. That's fine, as Yankelovich says, as far as it goes.
- **Steps two to four creep in.** Sometimes someone decides them, as Ford's management did. More often nobody rules that the support call that took an hour doesn't matter. It just never reaches the slide, and then it stops coming up.
- **A made-up number is worse than none.** Give "how much users love it" a score out of ten, and soon it's argued over as if someone measured it.
- **Name what you can't count, next to what you can.** In a review, ask "What matters here that this number can't see?" Then go and collect the stories for it.

## How to spot it

- A decision review shows only charts, and nobody asks what isn't on them.
- Something hard to count gets an invented score, then gets compared with real measures.
- "We don't have data on that" ends the discussion, when it should start the research.
- A feature that matters a lot to a small group, such as screen reader users on a banking app, gets waved off because the group barely shows in the totals.

## What it does not say

It doesn't say stop measuring. Yankelovich calls the first step okay, and in the same speech he says decisions worth millions shouldn't rest on hunches. The fallacy starts when what's easy to count crowds out the rest. It also isn't Goodhart's Law, in Module 2: that card is about a number that gets gamed once it becomes a target, and this one is about a number mistaken for the whole picture.

**One question to carry:** What matters here that none of our numbers can see, and who would know?

# The Law of Small Numbers

## The idea

Small samples swing more than anyone expects, experts included. In 1971, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman named the belief that a small sample will look like the whole population "the law of small numbers", a joke on the law of large numbers. They asked research psychologists how likely a study's result was to hold up in a small repeat of it. The median answer was 85 percent. A reasonable estimate, they showed, was about 48 percent.

A close relative is regression to the mean: an extreme result is usually followed by a less extreme one, whatever you did in between. Kahneman once told flight instructors that praise works better than punishment. A seasoned instructor pushed back: when he praised a cadet for a clean maneuver, the next try was usually worse, and when he screamed at one for a bad maneuver, the next try was usually better. Kahneman saw that both were regression to the mean. He called it a joyous moment, and summed it up: "we are statistically punished for rewarding others and rewarded for punishing them."

## Why a PM cares

- **Five test sessions find problems, not percentages.** Nielsen Norman Group's advice is that a handful of users in a usability test is enough to find the main problems in a design, and that putting a number on it takes many more. "Four of five liked it" isn't 80 percent of anyone.
- **Early results swing.** A test read after two days, or a pilot with three clinics, can look like a big win or a disaster by chance. The smaller the sample, the bigger the swing.
- **The worst week usually improves on its own.** Ship a fix after your worst week of failed payments, and the next week will probably look better anyway. Credit the fix only once you've compared it with something that didn't get it.
- **Your own experience is a small sample too.** The three customers you know best can feel like the whole picture.

## How to spot it

- A percentage quoted from a few interviews or a small pilot.
- A test stopped after a day or two because the early result looks strong.
- A team praised or blamed for one extreme month.
- "Every customer we've talked to" settles a question about all customers.

## What it does not say

It doesn't say small samples are useless. A few sessions with users are the right tool for finding what's broken and why. The trouble starts when a small sample is asked a big sample's question, such as how many or how often. And regression to the mean doesn't say nothing works. It says you need a comparison before you credit the change.

**One question to carry:** How many people is this based on, and would you believe it if it said the opposite?
