About 1 hour.

**If you only do one thing:** Take one decision in front of you, list the evidence you're leaning on, and write next to each piece who's missing from it.

## Why this matters

You'll meet both mistakes in the same week. On Monday, one vivid story from a customer, or one loud sales call, turns the roadmap. On Thursday, a dashboard settles an argument because it has decimals in it, though nobody can say who's in the data or who isn't. Both feel responsible at the time. The aim, in Andrew Chen's phrase, is to be data-informed, not data-driven: use the numbers and the stories for what each can tell you, and keep the decision yours.

Numbers tell you how much and how often, across many people, but only about what someone chose to measure. Stories tell you why, and what nobody thought to measure, but only about the people who told them. When the numbers and the stories disagree, look into why before you pick a side.

Your own hunch is evidence too. Trust it on two signals only: you've made this kind of call often, and each time you soon learned whether you were right (Kahneman and Klein, 2010).

## Do

**Weigh the evidence for one decision (25 minutes).** Pick a decision in front of you, such as whether a delivery app should change how it batches orders. List every piece of evidence you're leaning on, one line each, and mark each one a story or a number. Next to each, answer three questions in a few words. Who's missing from it? How was it collected? And what would have to hold here for it to carry over? Then write one sentence on what would change your mind.

**Optional: find the stories behind one number (10 minutes).** Pick a number on your dashboard that moved last month, such as the time to settle an insurance claim. Find three cases behind the move and read them. Does what happened to those people match what the number says? If not, write down what it isn't measuring.

## Check yourself

Answer in your head, then check.

Seven of the ten people in your usability test liked the new flow. Can you say 70 percent of your users like it?

**Answer:** No. Ten people picked for a test don't stand for all your users, and a percentage claims a precision the method can't give. Ask instead why the three who struggled struggled.

After your worst week of complaints, the team changes the sign-up flow, and the next week is better. Did the change work?

**Answer:** You can't tell yet. A worst week is usually followed by a better one anyway: that's regression to the mean. Compare with customers who didn't get the change (Module 6 covers how), or give it more weeks.

A competitor's case study says a feature lifted retention by 20 percent. What do you ask before you copy it?

**Answer:** Cartwright's question: how would it lead to that result here, step by step, and what would support or derail each step for your customers?

## For the glossary

- **McNamara fallacy:** Taking what's easy to measure for all that matters. The market researcher Daniel Yankelovich named it in 1971.

# The Law of Small Numbers

*Picture: A cadet's scores bouncing around their average, each praised peak followed by a dip.*

## The idea

Small samples swing more than anyone expects, experts included. In 1971, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman asked research psychologists how likely a study's result was to hold up in a small repeat. The median answer was 85 percent. A reasonable estimate, they showed, was about 48 percent. They called the belief that a small sample will look like the whole population "the law of small numbers".

Its close relative is regression to the mean: an extreme result is usually followed by a less extreme one, whatever you did in between. A flight instructor told Kahneman that cadets he praised for a clean maneuver usually did worse next time, and cadets he screamed at did better. Kahneman saw both as regression to the mean: "we are statistically punished for rewarding others and rewarded for punishing them."

## Why a PM cares

- **The worst week usually improves on its own.** Ship a fix after your worst week of app crashes, and the next week will probably look better anyway. Credit the fix only once you've compared it with something that didn't get it.
- **Your own experience is a small sample too.** The three customers you know best, or one loud sales call, can feel like the whole picture.

## How to spot it

- A percentage quoted from a few interviews or a small pilot.
- A team praised or blamed for one extreme month.

## What it does not say

It does not say small samples are useless. A few user sessions are the right tool for finding what's broken and why. They can't tell you how many or how often.

**One question to carry:** How many people is this based on, and would you believe it if it said the opposite?

# The McNamara Fallacy

*Picture: A bright dashboard, and around it in the dark what it can't count, such as an hour-long support call.*

## The idea

In 1971, the market researcher Daniel Yankelovich named "the McNamara fallacy", after Robert McNamara, president of Ford and then US Secretary of Defense. It runs in four steps:

1. "The first step is to measure whatever can be easily measured. This is okay as far as it goes."
2. "The second step is to disregard that which can't be easily measured or give it an arbitrary quantitative value. This is artificial and misleading."
3. "The third step is to presume that what can't be measured easily really isn't very important. This is blindness."
4. "The fourth step is to say that what can't be easily measured really doesn't exist. This is suicide."

## Why a PM cares

- **Steps two to four creep in.** Usually nobody rules that the hour-long support call doesn't matter. It never reaches your slides, and then it stops coming up.
- **A made-up number is worse than none.** Score "how much users love it" out of ten, and soon it's argued over as if someone measured it.

## How to spot it

- "We don't have data on that" ends the discussion, when it should start the research.
- Screen reader support on a banking app gets waved off because its users barely show in the totals.

## What it does not say

It does not say stop measuring. Yankelovich calls the first step okay, and says decisions worth millions shouldn't rest on hunches. Nor is it Goodhart's Law, from Module 2, a number gamed once it's a target. This one is a number mistaken for the whole picture.

**One question to carry:** What matters here that none of our numbers can see, and who would know?
