## Last time

Whom does your "in order to" name, and have you met them?

**Answer:** The people your work is for. If you can't name one you've talked to, or watched use the product, the vision rests on someone you've imagined.

**Answer:** That the rules serve the table's intent. Take the job as described first, then grow it toward value nobody assigned, where the written version stops serving the product.

## Why this matters

Own Your Role had you finish "We're building this in order to ...," a sentence that names someone the work is for. This module asks whether that person really has the problem, what it's worth to them, and how you'd know. Teams call this work discovery.

Start with what people do, not what they say they'd want. People are poor forecasters of their own behavior and good witnesses to their last week. Ask a woman what matters most when she buys jeans and she'll say the fit. Ask about the last pair she bought, and it came from Amazon, on sale, from a brand she knew. Your data shows what people did, but not why. So ask about the last time it happened, what they did about it, and what it cost them. The workaround is your real competition, and what it costs them is a ceiling on what your fix is worth.

Then ask whose problem it is. On a grocery delivery app, an adult child often orders for an aging parent: one person pays, another eats, and the app hears mostly from the one who pays. A gap like that pulls a team toward the buyer. Economists know a cousin of it as the principal-agent problem, where whoever acts for someone else drifts toward their own interests. On a platform team the gap is built in. If you run the service that picks substitutions, your immediate customer is the app team, and their problem is real, but it's borrowed from the parent whose milk was swapped for oat milk. Trace each request back to the experience it shows up in. GOV.UK's service guide counts the staff who run a service among its users for the same reason.

*Margin note: A tangent here, more obvious in B2B products: The customer is who buys, while the consumer is who experiences it. Be mindful when these two aren't the same, as it creates incentives to prioritize the former at the expense of the latter.*

Value alone doesn't order the work, because waiting costs money too. Cost of delay is what a week of waiting costs, and once you price it, the urgent and the merely valuable stop looking alike. At Maersk Line, one feature took 82 hours to build and 46 weeks to reach customers, at a cost of delay of roughly $210,000 a week. Find the Constraint puts the same price on the queue in front of the slowest step.

The customers who reach you aren't the ones who don't. An interview pool drawn from your keenest users hears from people like them, the error Weigh the Evidence warns about, and the people who left, or never came, have the problem you most need to hear. An order has a third person in it, too: the shopper who picks it and brings it to the door. The price and the delivery window rest on their work, and they're never the customer. In New York, a city study of the apps' own records found that restaurant delivery workers spent about two fifths of their working time waiting, unpaid, and that a large pool of waiting workers helps an app promise short windows and keep pay per trip low. An MIT audit of one grocery app's pay, from shoppers who sent in their own screenshots, found that more than a quarter of them had worked at least one shift below their state's minimum wage. The legal scholar Veena Dubal argues that on-demand companies design their systems to keep that pool large and its pay low.

Once building gets cheap, knowing what's worth building is most of the job. Google's People + AI Guidebook asks first whether AI is the right tool at all: "Often a rule or heuristic-based solution will work just as well, if not better, than an AI version." Where a model's judgment helps, and where a plain rule should stay in charge, is a product decision, made from the problem and not the tool.

## For the glossary

- **Cost of delay:** What a week of waiting costs, in the value you don't get while the work isn't done, which puts urgency and value in one number.
- **Jobs to be done:** Clayton Christensen's idea that people hire a product to make progress in a situation, so the situation predicts what they'll buy better than who they are.

# The Last Time

*Picture: two questions side by side. "Would you use a feature that ...?" brings back "Yes, probably," a forecast. "Tell me about the last time ..." brings back a story, drawn with the highlighter: when it happened, what they did instead, and what it cost them.*

## The idea

Ask about the last time it happened, not whether they'd want it. A forecast of your own behavior is a guess, and last Tuesday is evidence. Teresa Torres puts it plainly: "Our past behavior is much more predictive of our future behavior than any prediction we might make ourselves about our future behavior." Then ask what they did about it and what it cost them, which is what it's worth to them.

## Why a PM cares

- **"Would you" builds for the customer people imagine being.** A roadmap built on those answers gets features everyone said yes to and nobody uses.
- **The workaround is the competition.** The spreadsheet, the phone call, the second app: whatever they did last time is what your feature has to beat.
- **The cost is the price ceiling.** What the problem cost them last time, in money or hours, tells you what a fix is worth to them.

## How to spot it

- An interview question starts with "Would you," "Do you ever" or "How important is."
- A research summary says "users want" with no instance behind it.
- A survey asks people to predict what they'll pay.

## What it does not say

It doesn't say people's opinions are worthless. They tell you what someone believes about themselves, which matters for how you describe the product. They're just poor evidence of what they'll do.

**One question to carry:** When did this last happen, and what did you do?
