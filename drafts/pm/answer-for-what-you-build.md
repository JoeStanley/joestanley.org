About 64 minutes: 29 reading, 35 doing (a 25-minute exercise and a 10-minute optional one).

**If you only do one thing:** Run a consequence scan on one feature you own, and write down one change you'd propose.

## Why this matters

Your choices land on people who were never in the meeting. On a delivery platform, small rules decide how many stops fit in a shift, which order waits when a van runs late, and what counts as delivered. Across millions of orders, a small rule becomes a large effect.

So ask two things of what you build. Who gains, and who pays? A product can earn by helping people or by making them hard to leave, and for a while the two look alike on a dashboard. And when you feel like one cog among many, what's your share?

## The short path

1. **Doteveryone, ["Consequence Scanning"](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/resource/doteveryone-consequence-scanning/) (2019)**

   10 minutes. Free at adalovelaceinstitute.org. Read the page, then the manual's steps for running a session.

   **Look for:** The three questions you'll use in the Do, and how a session runs.

   **Why trust it:** It's from the method's designers at Doteveryone, a think tank that closed in 2020. It describes a tool rather than testing one.

   **What you'll get:** A conversation to run with your team in about an hour, before a feature is built.
2. **Fred Reichheld and Rob Markey, ["Blowing the whistle on bad profits"](https://bain.com/insights/blowing-whistle-on-bad-profits) (Bain & Company, 2006)**

   10 minutes. Free at bain.com.

   **Look for:** What they count as bad profits, "profits earned at the expense of customer relationships". Then ask how much of your product's revenue comes from value it created, and how much from making it hard to leave.

   **Why trust it:** A consultancy arguing for its own customer survey, so read it as a case for a method.

   **What you'll get:** The business case against capturing value, taking it from customers rather than creating it for them, ready for your next pricing review.
3. **Many Hands**

   Concept card. 2 minutes.

   **Look for:** The phrases people reach for to feel less responsible.
4. **Bruce Schneier, ["Data Is a Toxic Asset, So Why Not Throw It Out?"](https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2016/03/data_is_a_toxic_asse.html) (2016)**

   7 minutes. Free at schneier.com.

   **Look for:** Why he treats stored data as a liability. Then put one test to every field you collect: which decision will it change?

   **Why trust it:** A security writer's opinion essay, first published by CNN: an argument, not a study.

   **What you'll get:** A reason to collect less that rests on risk.

## Do

**Scan one feature you own (25 minutes).** Pick a feature, such as a retry rule for failed card payments or a marketplace search ranking. On one page, answer the method's three questions in order:

1. What are the intended and unintended consequences? Name everyone affected, including the easiest to overlook, like the cardholder whose retried payment tips the account into overdraft.
2. Which positive consequences do you want to focus on?
3. Which do you want to mitigate? Beside each one, write who'd own the fix.

End with one change you'd propose. Afterwards, show it to someone in support and ask what you missed.

**Optional: list what the feature keeps (10 minutes).** List each data field it collects, next to the decision that uses it, and mark any field with no decision. Then pick one field. If a customer asked you to erase it, could you find every copy, in the backups and at the companies you share it with?

## Check yourself

Answer in your head, then check.

A change lifts hours watched on your streaming service by starting the next episode without asking. Has it created value?

**Answer:** Ask whether viewers are better off, or only up later than they meant to be. If they'd switch it off given a plain choice, those hours were captured, not created.

To meet sales targets, Wells Fargo employees opened accounts customers hadn't asked for. Which idea from this module explains it, and which from Find the Constraint?

**Answer:** Many Hands: each employee could say they were only following the targets. And Goodhart's Law: once accounts opened became a target, the count stopped showing what customers wanted.

A teammate wants a new sign-up field "in case we need it". What do you ask?

**Answer:** Which decision will it change? If none does yet, leave it out. Every field you keep is something to secure, and something to find when a customer asks you to erase it.

## For the glossary

- **Consequence scanning:** A short team conversation before a feature is built, about everything it could cause, run on three questions from Doteveryone.
- **Bad profits:** Fred Reichheld and Rob Markey's name for profits earned at the expense of customer relationships.
- **Problem of many hands:** Dennis Thompson's name for harm that comes from so many small contributions that it's hard to say who's responsible.
- **Right to erasure:** A person's right, under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU and the UK, to ask an organization to delete their personal data, in some circumstances.

# Many Hands

*Picture: hands passing a parcel down a line, none of them watching where it lands.*

## The idea

In a large organization, harm often comes from many small contributions, and no one person decides it. Dennis Thompson called this the problem of many hands (1980): so many people contribute that it's hard to say who's responsible.

The psychologist Albert Bandura listed the ways people quiet their own conscience while they do harm, and the signs below follow his list.

## Why a PM cares

- **You're a hand, not a bystander.** Thompson asks whether your act was a cause, and whether you could have acted otherwise. "I had no choice" is often a poor excuse, and so is not having noticed.
- **Targets can turn people into cogs.** The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found that sales targets and pay incentives led Wells Fargo employees to open accounts without customers' consent.
- **Responsibility can be designed in.** Thompson proposes naming who's responsible for the structure before anything fails. For you, that's an owner for each harm you can foresee, written into the decision's record and said aloud in the meeting. That step is this syllabus's suggestion, not his.

## How to spot it

- Displacement: "The business asked for it."
- Diffusion: "It passed launch review, and everyone agreed."
- Soft labels: "engagement" for what users feel as pressure.
- Distance: the harm lands in a support queue you never see.

## What it does not say

It doesn't say you're as guilty as whoever made the decision, or that you have to refuse every request you dislike. Your share is usually to see the consequence, say it clearly and make sure someone owns it.

**One question to carry:** If this harms a customer, whose name would be on the decision, and is it anyone's yet?
