About 67 minutes: 32 reading, 35 doing (a 25-minute exercise and a 10-minute optional one).

**If you only do one thing:** Write three things your team won't do this quarter, each with a one-sentence reason.

## Why this matters

A roadmap is a set of decisions, and on most teams many of them get made by default: by adding, by deferring, or by never deciding. Make them on purpose. That means working out which decisions deserve care, and saying what you're choosing not to do. "The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do," as Michael Porter puts it. An analytics tool built both for analysts who want every control and for executives who want one chart serves neither well. Porter calls that straddling, and it's expensive.

Then ask whose decision it is. A few decisions need a view of the whole, and most are better made by the people doing the work. Either way, name one person to decide. Input shapes a decision, but it isn't a veto.

## The short path

1. **Jeff Bezos, ["2015 Letter to Shareholders"](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312516530910/d168744dex991.htm) (Amazon, 2016)**

   5 minutes. Free at sec.gov. Read the passage on one-way and two-way doors.

   **Look for:** Which decisions deserve the slow process: the one-way doors, hard or impossible to undo. Most decisions are two-way doors, and those deserve a quick call.

   **Why trust it:** A chief executive's rule for his own company, not a study.

   **What you'll get:** A test for any decision: how hard is it to undo?
2. **Agency and Coherence**

   Concept card. 2 minutes.

   **Look for:** What to hold strict, and when to take a decision away from a team.
3. **Andrew Harmel-Law, ["Scaling the Practice of Architecture, Conversationally"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/scaling-architecture-conversationally.html) (martinfowler.com, 2021)**

   10 minutes. Free at martinfowler.com. Read the section on the advice process.

   **Look for:** Anyone can decide, after asking everyone the decision affects and people with expertise in it. The decider is "in no way obliged to agree with the advice", but has to seek it out and write it down.

   **Why trust it:** A Thoughtworks architect on a practice he's used with clients.

   **What you'll get:** A way to spread decisions out and still know who's accountable.
4. **Richard Rumelt, ["The perils of bad strategy"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-perils-of-bad-strategy) (*McKinsey Quarterly*, 2011)**

   15 minutes. Free at mckinsey.com.

   **Look for:** The senior team that couldn't choose, and his kernel of a strategy: a diagnosis, a guiding policy that rules things out, and coherent action.

   **Why trust it:** A strategy professor at UCLA, arguing from the cases he's seen.

   **What you'll get:** Two questions for any strategy you're handed: does it say what's going on, and does it rule anything out?

## Do

**Write a kernel and a not-doing list (25 minutes).** Write one sentence for the diagnosis, the main challenge facing your product area, and one for the guiding policy, your approach to it. Add two or three actions that carry it out together. Then list three things you won't do this quarter, whether someone asked for them or your team does them now, each with a sentence tying the no to your diagnosis. Before you stop anything your team does now, find out why it started and what leans on it, as G. K. Chesterton advised before taking down any fence. That may take a conversation after the 25 minutes. Test each line: would a reasonable colleague argue for the opposite? If not, it isn't a choice yet.

**Optional: take apart a stalled decision (10 minutes).** Pick a decision from last quarter that stalled. Who had to be consulted, and who could say no? Was there one named decider, and a decision record? Write two lines on what the advice process would have changed.

## Check yourself

Answer in your head, then check.

Which needs the slow, careful process: a new sign-up flow piloted in one market, or a new field in a public API?

**Answer:** The API field. Once outside developers build on it, removing it breaks their code: it's nearly a one-way door, and the pilot isn't.

A decision has stalled because five people each think they can say no. What do you change?

**Answer:** Name one person to decide, and make everyone else an adviser. Nobody holds a veto.

Your team's strategy reads "Delight customers with a fast, reliable product," and nobody objects. What's wrong with it?

**Answer:** Nobody would argue for the opposite, so it isn't a choice. It's a goal, with no diagnosis or guiding policy behind it.

## For the glossary

- **One-way and two-way doors:** Jeff Bezos's terms for decisions you can't easily undo, and decisions you can.
- **Straddling:** Michael Porter's word for trying to hold two positions that don't fit together, such as a full-service airline copying a low-cost rival.
- **Advice process:** Anyone may make a decision, after asking the people it affects and people with relevant expertise. They don't have to follow the advice.
- **Decision record:** A short note of one significant decision: its context, the decision, its status and its consequences. Engineers call it an architecture decision record (ADR).
- **Kernel of a strategy:** Richard Rumelt's name for the three parts a strategy needs: a diagnosis of what's going on, a guiding policy that rules many actions out, and coordinated actions that carry it out.

# Agency and Coherence

*Picture: small arrows on paths of their own, inside one broad arrow.*

## The idea

Work many people do at once needs two things that pull against each other: room to decide, and pieces that fit. Jabe Bloom names both (2020). Requisite variety, after Ross Ashby's law, means a team needs views as varied as the world it faces. Requisite coherence means it also needs "just enough common concepts to make progress", and "not maximally but minimally".

The philosopher Alicia Juarrero shows how to have both. Constraints "also create alternatives": the rules of syntax are what let a sentence mean anything at all. So be strict about a few things, and loose about the rest.

## Why a PM cares

- **You often write the strict list.** Agree what "delivered" means, and a marketplace's teams can build without meeting about it. But take a decision from a team only for a benefit "that the organization could not otherwise achieve", as Ruth Malan and Dana Bredemeyer wrote in 2002.
- **Give the intent, not only the task.** The Marine Corps' 1997 doctrine, MCDP 1, seeks unity "not principally through imposed control, but through harmonious initiative". Knowing why, a team can adapt when the plan goes stale.
- **Agency needs feedback.** Bloom calls autonomy "the ability to choose which action to take". Agency adds seeing what the choice did, so get a team the numbers for its own change.

## How to spot it

- Teams ask permission for small changes.
- Every team's piece works and the whole doesn't.
- A team says it's doing well, and its neighbors disagree.

## What it does not say

It doesn't say more autonomy is always better. Guzzo and Dickson's 1996 review found "substantial variance" in what autonomous work groups did for productivity.

**One question to carry:** Does this decision need a view of the whole? If it doesn't, whose is it?
