## The idea

In a large organization, harm often comes from many small contributions, and no one person decides it. Dennis Thompson called this the {{< pm/term "many-hands" "problem of many hands" >}} (1980): so many people contribute that it's hard to say who's responsible.

The psychologist Albert Bandura listed the ways people quiet their own conscience while they do harm, and the signs below follow his list.

## Why a PM cares

- **You're a hand, not a bystander.** Thompson asks whether your act was a cause, and whether you could have acted otherwise. "I had no choice" is often a poor excuse, and so is not having noticed.
- **Targets can turn people into cogs.** The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found that sales targets and pay incentives led Wells Fargo employees to open accounts without customers' consent.
- **Responsibility can be designed in.** Thompson proposes naming who's responsible for the structure before anything fails. For you, that's an owner for each harm you can foresee, written into the decision's record and said aloud in the meeting. That step is this syllabus's suggestion, not his.

## How to spot it

- Displacement: "The business asked for it."
- Diffusion: "It passed launch review, and everyone agreed."
- Soft labels: "engagement" for what users feel as pressure.
- Distance: the harm lands in a support queue you never see.

## What it does not say

It doesn't say you're as guilty as whoever made the decision, or that you have to refuse every request you dislike. Your share is usually to see the consequence, say it clearly and make sure someone owns it.
