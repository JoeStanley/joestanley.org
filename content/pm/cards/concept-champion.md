## The idea

Kim Clark's "concept" is what you'd call the vision. A product hangs together when someone champions it. Clark and colleagues studied 29 car projects in the 1980s. Most had a lightweight manager, who coordinates "but has little influence over the content of the project". The few with a {{< pm/term "heavyweight" >}}, "not only a coordinator but a concept champion", used far fewer engineering hours. Fred Brooks calls a product that hangs together one with {{< pm/term "conceptual-integrity" >}}.

Set the vision as a destination, keep it current as engineers and customers teach you, and work back from it.

## Why a PM cares

- **Product management isn't project management.** A project can be on time and on budget and still be the wrong product.
- **You facilitate, but you aren't a facilitator.** A facilitator stays out of the content, and you can't. Groups picked the best candidate 18 percent of the time when the facts for it were spread among them, and 83 percent when everyone had them (Stasser and Titus, 1985).
- **The vision needs a voice of its own.** Tolstoy saw art as joining people "together in the same feelings", and strongest when it's individual and sincere. In *Mozart in the Jungle*, that's playing with the blood.

## How to spot it

- You can say who asked for each roadmap item, but not what the product is for.
- Time, budget and scope are all on track, and nobody can say what changed for users.
- Your week is status updates and meetings.

## What it does not say

It doesn't make you a lone genius. Brooks writes that "often the fresh concept does come from an implementer or from a user."
