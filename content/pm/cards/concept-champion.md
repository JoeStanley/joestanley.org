## The idea

Kim Clark's "concept" is what you'd call the vision. A product hangs together, what Fred Brooks calls {{< pm/term "conceptual-integrity" >}}, when someone champions it. Clark and colleagues studied 29 car projects in the 1980s. Most had a lightweight manager, who coordinates "but has little influence over the content of the project." The few with a {{< pm/term "heavyweight" >}}, "not only a coordinator but a concept champion," used far fewer engineering hours.

Set the vision as a destination, keep it current as engineers and customers teach you, and work back from it.

## Why a PM cares

- **Product management isn't project management.** A project can be on time and on budget and still be the wrong product.
- **You facilitate, but you aren't a facilitator.** You can't stay out of the content, since groups don't pool what they know on their own: they picked the best candidate 18 percent of the time when its facts were spread among them, 83 percent when everyone had them ([Stasser and Titus, 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.48.6.1467)).
- **The vision needs a voice of its own.** People respond to the person in the work. A product made by averaging every request can be right in every detail and still say nothing.

## How to spot it

- You can say who asked for each roadmap item, but not what the product is for.
- Time, budget and scope are all on track, and nobody can say what changed for users.
- Your week is status updates and meetings.

## What it does not say

It doesn't make you a lone genius. Brooks writes that "often the fresh concept does come from an implementer or from a user."
