## The idea

A big enough mission can excuse almost anything. When a founder's vision promises to cure disease, end scarcity or carry people to Mars, any cost in its way looks small, and any doubt looks like disloyalty. The psychologist Albert Bandura called this {{< pm/term "moral-justification" "moral justification" >}}: harm made acceptable by "portraying it as serving socially worthy or moral purposes." The costs are paid now, by real people. The vision may never arrive.

## Why a PM cares

- **You see the trade first.** You're often the one who knows the test failed, the review got skipped or which users a launch will hurt. The pull lands hardest on whoever knows.
- **Doubt gets turned on you.** At Theranos, Erika Cheung raised concerns about test results and was told to do what she was paid to do. Soon she wondered if she was "the crazy one."
- **Your line has to exist before the pitch.** Decide what you won't do for any mission while nobody is asking, and who outside your team you'd tell if someone asked.

## How to spot it

- Cosmic stakes: saving lives, saving humanity, the future itself.
- Inevitability: the future is coming anyway, and doubters stand in its way.
- Dissent read as disloyalty: "Dissent is resistance, to be overcome."
- Doubt turned inward: you start to think the problem is you.

## What it does not say

It doesn't say ambition is wrong, or that every founder runs a cult. Most missions are worth working for. A mission worth believing in can stand a question about its costs.
