## The idea

The {{< pm/term "trust-equation" "trust equation" >}} is a model of why people trust an adviser, and that includes you. It sets out four parts as a fraction: credibility, reliability and intimacy, added together, divided by self-orientation.

- **Credibility** is what you say: can you be believed?
- **Reliability** is what you do: do you meet expectations?
- **Intimacy** is whether people feel safe being vulnerable with you.
- **Self-orientation** is where your focus sits: on yourself, or on the other person and a shared goal.

The model first appeared in *The Trusted Advisor* (2000), by David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford.

## Why a PM cares

- **Reliability is what a rhythm builds.** An update that arrives on the day you said it would is a small promise kept.
- **Self-orientation is the bottom of the fraction.** An update that lists only wins reads as being about you. Name the problem you're watching, and it turns toward the reader.
- **Credibility needs some bad news.** If every report says everything's fine, the good news stops being believed. One honest item that isn't on track makes the rest easier to trust.

## How to spot it

- Your updates say "I" and "we" far more than "you need" or "you'll want to know."
- Someone else tells your manager about a problem before you do.

## What it does not say

It's a consultants' model, drawn from their practice rather than measured. Researchers split trustworthiness differently, into ability, benevolence and integrity, and treat trust itself as a willingness to be vulnerable (Mayer, Davis and Schoorman, 1995). Use the equation as a checklist for one relationship.
