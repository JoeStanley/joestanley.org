## The idea

Every idea rests on things that have to be true: people want it, it pays, the team can build it, people can use it, and it harms no one. List them, and place each by how much the idea depends on it and how much evidence you have. Test first those "critical to the success of our idea where we have little evidence that suggests that they are safe," as Teresa Torres puts it, with the smallest test that could prove one wrong. A sketch shows whether people can use it. Whether they want it takes something they act on, such as a {{< pm/term "fake-door" "fake door" >}}. Decide first what result would stop you.

## Why a PM cares

- **The riskiest one isn't always demand.** The UK's Register to vote service depended on reaching hundreds of local councils' systems, so its team worked on that first ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-alpha-phase-works)).
- **A small test can sink an assumption, but not size it.** If none of five shoppers can finish a two-store order, you've learned something. If four of five tap the button, you don't yet know how many customers will ([The Law of Small Numbers](/pm/cards/law-of-small-numbers/)).

## How to spot it

- The first test is of how a screen looks, not whether anyone wants it.
- Nobody can say what result would stop the project.
- "We'll find out when we launch."

## What it does not say

It doesn't say a passed test proves the idea: it rules out one way to fail. A test shows what people did, not why, and only for a while, since new things get tapped because they're new ([Kohavi and colleagues, 2009](https://ai.stanford.edu/~ronnyk/2009controlledExperimentsOnTheWebSurvey.pdf)). A tap on a fake door is interest, not use. Tell people right away it isn't ready.
