> You don't become a good {{< pm/edit "Communist" "Product Manager" >}} by going to meetings or memorizing the [Manifesto](http://agilemanifesto.org). You do it on the streets. You do it with your fists. The rest is bullshit and you know it.
>
> [Gregor Anghel](https://youtu.be/60P5qYQEuIw?t=43)

[Comrade Detective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comrade_Detective) was an over-the-top delight, but there's some truth in Detective Anghel's words. In my experience, good product managers became good by accumulating a broad set of experiences across jobs, companies, and types of products. They can then bring the lessons from that experience to their work in unexpected ways. But where I and the good detective part ways is that the truly best product managers I've worked with went on to couple that praxis with theory.

This isn't a substitute for the experience. You'll get that in time.

It's not a list of tools or frameworks. You can find those anywhere.

Instead, it's a set of lessons intended to strengthen your **{{< pm/term "product-judgment" >}}**, supplementing what you'll learn on the job with the principles behind the work. Put another way, it reinforces your internal compass to navigate situations for which you have no map, showing you which questions to get answered before you make a call, on matters where there's rarely one right answer.

This curriculum is intended for early- to mid-career product managers, and draws its examples from a mix of industries. The more senior you are within that spectrum, the more you've likely learned these lessons in part. If you're a senior product leader, I hope this material resonates enough that you share it with the intended PMs in your org.

## How it works

- **Thirteen modules, each built on one question, then a closing page.** A module takes up to 90 minutes: roughly an hour of reading, watching or playing, and the rest applying it to your own work. Reading the module's own page, writing its log line and answering its questions adds about 20 minutes, so plan on about two hours a week. At a module a week, the whole course takes a quarter.

- **Starting with {{< pm/mod "know-who-its-for" >}}, you'll keep a decision log:** one call a module, what you expect to happen, how sure you are as a percentage, and when you'll know. Intuition earns trust where a situation has patterns you can learn and you get clear feedback on your calls ([Kahneman and Klein, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755)). Product calls can take months to play out, so where you can, log one you'll know about within the quarter. The closing page, [The Compass](/pm/the-compass/), has you look back over it, and names [what the course leaves out](/pm/the-compass/#what-the-course-leaves-out). It may feel silly, but the log is one of the most important parts of the entire curriculum: making it real in a place you can safely fail.

- **Some exercises end with a question for a colleague.** That part sits outside the module's time. Ideally, tell a few of your engineering and business colleagues in advance that you're doing these modules, and that you may come to them along the way for feedback on tasks that touch their work. Most people welcome a product manager who wants to become a better working partner, and it gives you an excuse to deepen those relationships, if you need one. Where the answers can wait, save them up for a month and ask each person theirs in one conversation.

- **Every module has a short path that's free to read.** Books are on the full path, which is optional. Take it when a question keeps coming up in your work.

- **Every source says why to trust it,** starting with its kind of evidence: a study, a review of studies, experience or an argument. It says what you'll get from it, too, so you can decide what's worth your time.

  The exception to this is one story per module, usually a movie or a show, which conveys the module's concepts in some way. While fictional or dramatized, a single compelling story is more memorable than a stack of research.

- **Each idea worth keeping gets a concept card, one page long.** Keep them near your work.

- **On the site, your progress stays in your browser.** Nothing is sent or tracked anywhere by me. Tick each step of a short path as you finish it. Its last two are the Do and the Check, so a module reads Done once you've done everything in it but the full path. How well you did them is yours to judge.

This is largely a synthesis of the work of others that has guided me, and the curation is me trying to pay it forward. If you disagree with any of this, especially on the merits, tell me why at {{< pm/email >}}. The same goes for a broken link, or a part that lost you.
