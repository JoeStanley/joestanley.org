# The syllabus's voice

How On Product Judgment should sound. Read this before you write or revise any unit. The rules a check can hold are in `tests/SPEC.md` (E1 to E4); this page is the reasoning and the examples behind them.

It covers the syllabus only. The front door, the ReadMe and Writing are Joe's own voice and are his to write. So are the margin notes, where a session fixes only typos and house rules: they're meant to be looser, more casual and tangential than the rest, so this guide doesn't apply to them. Inside a unit, though, his own sentences and suggestions are a direction: revise them in minor ways where that makes the unit hold together or read more clearly, and ask him where you hesitate. He found earlier sessions keeping his exact words at the cost of the whole (21 November 2026).

## Who is talking

A mentor a few years ahead of you, across a table, with coffee. They have done the job and read the research, they like you, and they would rather you think than agree. They are not selling anything and they are not grading you.

Joe asked for this on 20 November 2026: the modules were "a degree too calm and reserved", and should feel "a little more casual, like a mentor conveying advice to a mentee in a coffee conversation", fitted to a structured program.

## Three volumes of one voice

- **The Start page is Joe at full volume.** First person, a point of view, a quote with a swear word in it. He writes it.
- **A module is the same person, one notch down, now teaching.** Warm and direct, organized, never in a hurry. This is where most of the words are.
- **A card is a half notch below that.** You keep it by your desk, so it is compact. It still sounds spoken.

The glossary is plain definitions. A reader should be able to go from the Start page into Module 1 without feeling that someone else took over.

## The rules

1. **Talk to "you".** Every module speaks to the reader directly.
2. **No "I" outside the Start page and the margin notes.** A mentor says "I", but only Joe can say what Joe has seen. Never invent an experience, an anecdote or an opinion for him. Where the text needs a view, state it plainly and let it stand.
3. **Use contractions the way a person talking does.** It's, don't, you'll, isn't, can't, won't, doesn't, that's, there's, you're, what's, here's. Most of the time, not every time: keep the full form where you would stress it out loud ("It does not say measures are bad"). Skip the ones that look odd in print (would've, it'd, there're, mustn't). Never touch a quotation or a title.
4. **Point first, then the reason.** Commit to the advice: do this, start here, skip that. Then say why, and say when it does not apply. Where something is hard to do, say so, and say what makes it hard.
5. **Name the usual mistake, and be kind about it.** "Most teams do this. It feels right." A mentee who hears that the mistake is normal keeps listening.
6. **Be concrete.** Every idea gets an example from real product work, and the examples come from different kinds of products (spec S5).
7. **Be straight about evidence.** Say when something is a study, a case, a practitioner's view or a hunch. "Why trust it" stays candid, including about a source's limits.
8. **Vary the sentences.** Mostly short. Let one run when the thought needs it. Three short sentences in a row read as a drumbeat.
9. **Keep the size.** The voice changes the words, not the length. A module still prints on three pages and a card on one (spec P1).
10. **Show what people do.** Start an example from one small, specific thing: the pull request that sat in review for nine days, not "delays". Describe a skill by what someone does, not by a trait. "She's strategic" gives the reader nothing to copy; "she asked which of the three launches could slip" does.
11. **Give everyone their real reasons.** The engineer who pushes back, the sales lead who wants a date, the lawyer who says no: each gets the reason they would give, in their own terms. No one in an example is a straw man, so the reader practices against the strongest case.
12. **Judge exactly, and praise in few words.** "The clearest short account of it we found" tells the reader more than "essential". No superlatives or hype about a source, a method or the syllabus itself.
13. **Let a number carry a fact, not weight.** Machine prose leans on exact figures to sound important: "across 142 studies", "over 23,000 responses". Say it the way a person would across the table: "in more than a hundred studies", "years of survey research". Keep the number where it's the point: the 43 minutes an error budget leaves, a third of the time against almost never, the 99th percentile to ask for. Joe asked for this on 22 November 2026, in the spirit of his rounded times.

## What it is not

- Not chatty. No jokes for their own sake, no exclamation marks, no "let's dive in", no "here's the thing".
- Not breezy about difficulty. Leave out "simply", "just" and "obviously" when they wave a hard thing away.
- Not management-speak. No "leverage" as a verb, "stakeholder alignment", "drive outcomes", "best-in-class".
- Not the habits of machine prose: the "not X, but Y" turn used by reflex, lists of three by reflex, a one-line moral closing every paragraph, a trailing "-ing" phrase tacked onto a sentence, an abstract noun where a verb would do.
- Not uniform. Every bullet in a list built the same way, or every paragraph closing on a short sentence, reads as a pattern instead of a person ("Across a list", below).
- Not preachy about judgment. The syllabus is about product judgment, and each module is one side of it. Say so where it helps, at most once in a module. The word is not a refrain.

Carried over from the standing rules: American English, with its punctuation, so a comma or a period that ends a quotation goes inside the closing mark (spec T9); no em dashes (spec T3); plain and short.

## Across a list

A line that reads well alone can tire a reader who meets the same build ten times in a row. The syllabus is mostly lists (sources, steps, a card's bullets), so this matters here more than anywhere.

- **Keep the label, free the fill.** Repeat exactly what the reader scans for: "Why trust it", "What you'll get", a step's title. Let the words after the label take the shape their content needs. Before you word a list, decide what each entry says, then give neighbors different builds: a fragment, one sentence, two.
- **Spend the turn.** A line that ends on a twist ("and that's the problem", "not X, but Y") works once. End most lines where their thing is, on the fact, the act or the advice, and keep the turn for the line that needs it. A paragraph that closes on a short, punchy sentence is the same habit.
- **Leave plain ground.** Let some lines only say what's there or what to do. When every line is worked to the same finish, the reader can't tell which one matters. Use the short sentence as a beat, not as the default.

## Source entries

Joe found the source entries "overly explained", in a way common to machine prose, on 21 November 2026. An entry is a recommendation you'd share over coffee: what it is, why it's neat or worth the time, and why it's worth trusting. Keep the labels (spec S2) and simplify what's in them. The *Ocean's Eleven* entry in Decide and Set Direction is his model.

- **Say what it is in a word or two,** "A book.", "A journal article.", "A TV series.", and on the full path stop there: no page count, no running time (spec S13). The short path's times stay, because they add up to the module's total.
- **No edition where the context makes it clear.** A reader with another edition still gets the lesson.
- **Never tell the reader to borrow or buy a book.** Saying where it's free is worth a few words.
- **Point to the part that matters,** the chapter, the scene, the episode, and let "What you'll get" say why it's worth it.
- **"Why trust it" is a sentence or two:** who made it and what kind of evidence it is. Leave out what doesn't change whether to trust it.

## How to revise a unit

Write a sentence again from what it says. Do not swap a word for its neighbor. A voice pass moves no fact, name, number, date, link or quotation, and adds none. Someone who did not write the pass reads it afterwards, as a whole, for whether it sounds like one person.

- **Read it cold first,** as the reader who will use it. Fix what it says and in what order before you touch a word: what comes first, what's missing, what the reader doesn't need. Every sentence spends the reader's attention, so cut the ones that buy nothing.
- **When a word doesn't land,** look first at how its paragraph is built. The word is rarely the cause.
- **Watch for the drift.** A model revising prose moves it the same way every time: fewer contractions and small words, longer words, more semicolons, colons and parentheses. Set a revision beside its draft and check for that drift before you keep it.
