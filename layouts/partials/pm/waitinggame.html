    What to take away

    1. Order the work by the estimates: divide each job's cost of delay by its weeks of work, and build the highest first.
    2. Test only an estimate whose answer could change that order. The checkout fix's range and reorder's are too narrow. Swap approvals' could go either way: if it flops it should go last, and if it works, right after the fix.
    3. Test while the team builds, and make each pick when the team needs it: its last responsible moment, the latest you can make it without losing an option. Here, stopping the team to find out cost more than any answer saved.

    What the game leaves out. It gives you the odds, and a free test that settles the question just as the team needs the next pick. At work you guess the odds, and tests cost time and answer less. And here a week of waiting costs the same all year. A job for the holidays earns little until the season, so it can wait for its own last responsible moment, but not a week past it. Real calls seldom come this tidy: when the answer is obvious, someone has usually made the call before it reaches you.

    Black Swan Farming, whose paper on the shipping company Maersk Line is on the full path, calls the rule CD3, cost of delay divided by duration. The last responsible moment, a term from the Lean Construction Institute, is "the moment at which failing to make a decision eliminates an important alternative," in Mary and Tom Poppendieck's Lean Software Development (2003).
