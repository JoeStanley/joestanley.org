What to take away

  1. Every week a job waits costs what it would have earned, so the waiting adds up for everything in the queue.
  2. Neither the biggest job first nor the quickest is cheapest here. Divide each job's cost of delay by its weeks of work, and build the highest first.
  3. The prices are guesses, and still worth making. A rough price on waiting settles what an argument about urgency can't.

What the game leaves out. Its numbers are certain, and at work none are. Here the order holds even if the checkout fix is worth half what you think, or swap approvals take twelve weeks, because the scores are far apart. Where two scores are close, check the estimates before you trust the order. A job can fail outright, too, and one that might is worth less than its price. Real calls seldom come this tidy: when the answer is obvious, someone has usually made the call before it reaches you.

The rule has a name on Black Swan Farming, where Joshua Arnold writes: CD3, cost of delay divided by duration, and "the higher the resulting score, the higher the priority." His paper with Özlem Yüce, on the full path below, tells how Maersk Line put a price on waiting.
