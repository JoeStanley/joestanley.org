What to take away

  1. Both ends of a ranking by rate belong to the smallest groups, by chance alone.
  2. The next week they trade places, so help for the worst looks as if it worked, and praise for the best as if it backfired.
  3. A real problem can sit in the middle of the ranking, at a big store, where it costs the most. Judge a rate against its size, as the funnel does, before you rank.
  4. A fix tried in a few small stores risks little, but each store's week is noisy. Pool their orders, compare them with small stores that didn't get it, give it weeks, and never judge it by rank.

What the game leaves out. Real stores differ in more than size. Their neighborhoods and their staff move a late rate too, so the funnel is a first filter, not a verdict. In the trial the two groups differ only in the fix. At work the stores that try one first are often the ones whose managers asked for it, so compare them with stores like them.

Comparing 52 fertility clinics in 1998, Clare Marshall and David Spiegelhalter found that ranks are "extremely unreliable statistical summaries of performance," above all for the smaller clinics. Spiegelhalter later made the case for funnel plots, which "avoid spurious ranking of institutions into 'league tables'" (Statistics in Medicine, 2005).
